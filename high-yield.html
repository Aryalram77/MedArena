🧠Brainstem Syndromes & Mnemonics

1Fundamental Rules of Brainstem Localization

A. The "Rule of 4" (Cranial Nerve Levels)

The 12 cranial nerves are divided across the brainstem:

  • Midbrain (Upper): Cranial Nerves 3 and 4
    (CN 1 and 2 originate directly from the forebrain/diencephalon).
  • Pons (Middle): Cranial Nerves 5, 6, 7, and 8
  • Medulla (Lower): Cranial Nerves 9, 10, 11, and 12

B. The "Rule of 12" (Medial Cranial Nerves)

  • Cranial nerves whose numbers divide evenly into 12 are located medially:

3,4,6,12

  • 3×4=12
  • 4×3=12
  • 6×2=12
  • 12×1=12
  • All other cranial nerves (5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11) exit laterally.

C. The "Rule of M" (Medial Brainstem Structures)

Everything located in the medial aspect starts with M:

  • Motor Cranial Nerves:
    • CN 3, 4, 6: Eye movement
    • CN 12: Tongue movement
  • Motor Pathway:
    • Corticospinal Tract (CST): Lesion causes contralateral hemiplegia / hemiparesis.
  • Medial Lemniscus (Dorsal Column Pathway):
    • Carries conscious proprioception, vibration, and fine touch
    • → Lesion causes contralateral loss of proprioception/vibration.
  • Medial Longitudinal Fasciculus (MLF):
    • Coordinates horizontal conjugate eye movements.

D. The "Rule of S" (Side / Lateral Brainstem Structures)

Everything located on the side (lateral) aspect starts with S:

  • Sensory Cranial Nerves:
    • CN 5 (trigeminal – facial sensation), CN 7, 8 (auditory/vestibular), CN 9, 10, 11.
  • Spinothalamic Tract (SPT):
    • Carries pain and temperature
    • → Crosses early in spinal cord
    • → Lesion causes contralateral loss of pain and temperature over the body.
  • Sympathetic Pathway:
    • Disruption causes ipsilateral Horner syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis).
  • Spinocerebellar / Cerebellar Pathways:
    • Disruption causes ipsilateral cerebellar ataxia.

E. General Deficit Lateralization

  • Cranial Nerve deficits are always IPSILATERAL to the lesion.
  • Long tract motor/sensory deficits (CST / Spinothalamic) are CONTRALATERAL.

Crossed Sensory Deficit (Alternating Hemianesthesia)

  • Seen in lateral brainstem lesions.
  • Ipsilateral face sensory loss (CN 5 spinal tract/nucleus).
  • Contralateral body pain & temperature loss (Spinothalamic tract).

2Midbrain Syndromes

All midbrain vascular syndromes involve an ipsilateral 3rd cranial nerve (Oculomotor) palsy and are typically supplied by penetrating branches of the Posterior Cerebral Artery (PCA).

Midbrain Syndromes = Ipsilateral CN 3 Palsy + Characteristic Feature

1. Weber Syndrome

  • Localization: Ventral midbrain / Cerebral peduncle.
  • Mnemonic: Weber = Weakness.

Features

  • Ipsilateral CN 3 palsy.
  • Contralateral hemiparesis / hemiplegia (involvement of Corticospinal Tract in cerebral peduncle).

2. Benedikt Syndrome

  • Localization: Midbrain tegmentum involving the Red Nucleus.
  • Mnemonic: Bened = Red nucleus; Benedikt = Bobbing head / involuntary movements.

Features

  • Ipsilateral CN 3 palsy.
  • Contralateral tremor, involuntary choreiform/athetoid movements, or ataxia (dentatorubrothalamic tract / red nucleus).

3. Nothnagel Syndrome

  • Localization: Superior Cerebellar Peduncle (SCP).
  • Mnemonic: North = Superior cerebellar peduncle.

Features

  • Ipsilateral CN 3 palsy.
  • Cerebellar ataxia (loss of coordination).

4. Claude Syndrome

  • Formula:

B+N=C

(Benedikt + Nothnagel = Claude).

  • Localization: Involves both the Red Nucleus and Superior Cerebellar Peduncle.

Features

  • Ipsilateral CN 3 palsy.
  • Contralateral extrapyramidal signs/tremor (Benedikt component).
  • Cerebellar ataxia (Nothnagel component).

5. Parinaud Syndrome (Dorsal Midbrain / Tectal Syndrome)

  • Etiology: Pineal gland tumor / germinoma compressing the dorsal midbrain (tectum/colliculi).
  • Mnemonic: Parinaud = Pineal tumor / Dorsal midbrain.
  • Involvement: Superior colliculi and pretectal area.

Features

  • Upward gaze palsy (vertical gaze center affected; "sunset sign").
  • Convergence-retraction nystagmus.
  • Lid retraction (Collier sign).
  • Light-near dissociation (pseudo-Argyll Robertson pupil: reacts to accommodation but not to light).

3Pontine Syndromes

Pontine cranial nerves are 5, 6, 7, and 8 (CN 6 is medial; CN 5, 7, 8 are lateral).

1. Marie-Foix Syndrome (Lateral Pontine Syndrome)

  • Vascular Supply: AICA (Anterior Inferior Cerebellar Artery, branch of the Basilar artery).
  • Mnemonic: Foix = Facial (CN 7) + hearing loss (CN 8).
    (Also: "AICA" sounds like the Marathi word for "listen", linking AICA to CN 8 / hearing).

Features

  • Ipsilateral facial weakness/paralysis (CN 7).
  • Ipsilateral hearing loss, tinnitus, vertigo, nystagmus (CN 8 / labyrinthine artery).
  • Ipsilateral loss of facial sensation (CN 5).
  • Contralateral hemiplegia (CST).
  • Contralateral loss of pain and temperature on body (SPT).
  • Ipsilateral limb ataxia.
  • Ipsilateral Horner syndrome.

3. Raymond Syndrome

  • Mnemonic: Ray = X-ray
  • → X = Roman numeral VI (6 only).

Features

  • Ipsilateral CN 6 palsy (lateral rectus weakness).
  • Contralateral hemiparesis (CST).
  • Spares CN 7.

4. Locked-in Syndrome

  • Etiology: Acute bilateral ventral pontine infarction due to Basilar artery thrombosis.
  • Mnemonic: Locked in the Basement
  • → Basilar artery occlusion.

Features

  • Complete quadriplegia and anarthria/aphonia (loss of all voluntary motor function).
  • Preserved consciousness (Reticular Activating System in tegmentum is intact).
  • Preserved vertical eye movements and blinking (supranuclear centers in midbrain spared).

4Medullary Syndromes

Medullary cranial nerves are 9, 10, 11, and 12 (CN 12 is medial; CN 9, 10, 11 are lateral).

Medulla:
- Medial: CN 12 + CST + Medial Lemniscus
- Lateral: CN 9, 10, 11 + CN 5 + Spinothalamic + Sympathetic + Cerebellar

1. Medial Medullary Syndrome (Dejerine Syndrome)

  • Vascular Supply: Anterior Spinal Artery (ASA) or branch of Vertebral Artery.
  • Mnemonic: MD = Medial is Dejerine.

Features

  • Ipsilateral CN 12 palsy: Weakness and atrophy of the tongue.
    • Mnemonic: "Tongue licks the wound"
    • → Tongue deviates toward the side of the lesion.
  • Contralateral hemiplegia: Corticospinal tract involvement.
  • Contralateral loss of proprioception/vibration: Medial lemniscus involvement.

(Note: Do not confuse Dejerine Medullary Syndrome with Dejerine-Roussy Syndrome, which is a thalamic pain syndrome).

2. Lateral Medullary Syndrome (Wallenberg Syndrome)

  • Vascular Supply: Vertebral Artery (→ Posterior Inferior Cerebellar Artery / PICA).
  • Mnemonic: PICA-chew
  • → Chewing/swallowing/speech difficulty (CN 9 & 10).

Structures Involved

  • Nucleus Ambiguus (CN 9, 10, 11): Dysphagia, dysarthria, hoarseness, loss of gag reflex, uvula deviates to the opposite side.
  • Spinal Trigeminal Tract/Nucleus (CN 5): Ipsilateral facial loss of pain & temperature.
  • Spinothalamic Tract: Contralateral body loss of pain & temperature.
  • Vestibular Nuclei: Vertigo, nausea, vomiting, nystagmus.
  • Inferior Cerebellar Peduncle: Ipsilateral limb ataxia.
  • Descending Sympathetics: Ipsilateral Horner syndrome.

Critical Negative Findings

  • NO hemiplegia (CST is medial).
  • NO tongue deviation (CN 12 is medial).

5Clinical Integration: Taste Pathway

Clinical Question:
Which brainstem syndrome causes loss of taste from the anterior 2/3 of the tongue?

Innervation Pathway

  • Taste from anterior 2/3 of tongue
  • → CN 7 (Chorda tympani).
  • Taste from posterior 1/3 of tongue
  • → CN 9.
  • Taste from epiglottis/vallecula
  • → CN 10.
  • All taste fibers terminate in the Nucleus Tractus Solitarius (NTS) (N = 9, T = 10, S = 7).

Answer

  • Because the anterior 2/3 taste is mediated by CN 7, it is impaired in Lateral Pontine Syndrome (Marie-Foix syndrome / AICA occlusion).

6Internuclear Ophthalmoplegia (INO) & the MLF

Anatomy of Horizontal Gaze

  • To look to one side (e.g., to the left):
    • Left CN 6 nucleus activates the Left Lateral Rectus (abduction).
    • Left CN 6 nucleus simultaneously sends an interneuron through the Right MLF across the midline to the Right CN 3 subnucleus (Medial Rectus).
    • Right Medial Rectus adducts the right eye.
Right Eye (Adduction)  <--- [Right MLF] <--- [Left CN 6 Nucleus] ---> Left Eye (Abduction)
(CN 3)                                                              (CN 6)

Pathophysiology of INO

  • The MLF is named by the adduction deficit:

Right MLF Lesion

  • When asked to look to the Left:
    • Right eye cannot adduct (failure of right medial rectus).
    • Left eye abducts with nystagmus (compensatory overactivation).

Primary Gaze

  • Mnemonic: I-NO = In primary gaze, NO abnormality (eyes look straight ahead with no squint).

Etiology

  • Young patients / Bilateral INO: Multiple Sclerosis (demyelinating plaque in MLF).
  • Elderly patients / Unilateral INO: Brainstem Stroke (ischemia to paramedian pontine perforators of basilar artery).

3Frontal Eye Field & Summary of Key Brodmann Areas

📝 Exam Check

Frontal eye field is Brodmann area:

  1. A. 4
  2. B. 8
  3. C. 41, 42
  4. D. 17
✔ Answer: B. 8

High-Yield Brodmann Areas & Mnemonics

Frontal Cortex Motor Areas (Even Numbers):
Area 4 (Primary Motor) ---> Area 6 (Premotor) ---> Area 8 (Frontal Eye Field)

Frontal Eye Field (Area 8)

  • Mnemonic: Eye field looks like a pair of glasses/spectacles
  • → shaped like the number 8.
  • Cross-topic pearl: Mahatma Gandhi (MG = Myasthenia Gravis) wore round spectacles
  • → HLA-B8 association.

Primary Sensory Cortex (Areas 3, 1, 2)

  • Postcentral gyrus (Parietal lobe).
  • Sensation comes first before movement
  • → Count 1, 2, 3.

Primary Motor Cortex (Area 4)

  • Precentral gyrus (Frontal lobe).
  • Motor control of our 4 limbs
  • → Area 4.

Premotor / Supplementary Motor Cortex (Area 6)

  • Located anterior to Area 4.

Primary Visual Cortex (Area 17)

  • Located in the occipital lobe (calcarine sulcus).
  • Mnemonic: Seeing is Visual
  • → Seeing = Seventeen (Area 17).

4Quadrigeminal Cistern & Vein of Galen

📝 Exam Check

Quadrigeminal cistern contains:

  1. A. Occipital artery
  2. B. Middle cerebral artery
  3. C. Vein of Galen
  4. D. Superior sagittal sinus
✔ Answer: C. Vein of Galen

Anatomy of the Cistern

  • Boundaries: Located dorsal to the midbrain colliculi (quadrigeminal plate / tectum) and inferior to the splenium of the corpus callosum.
  • Content: Great Cerebral Vein of Galen.
  • Venous Drainage Pathway:
Two Internal Cerebral Veins (ICVs)+Basal Veins (of Rosenthal) → Vein of Galen → Straight SinusTwo Internal Cerebral Veins (ICVs)+Basal Veins (of Rosenthal) → Vein of Galen → Straight Sinus

High-Yield Clinical Pearl: Vein of Galen Malformation (VGAM)

  • An intracranial arteriovenous fistula (AVF) causing dilatation of the median prosencephalic vein.

Classic Presentation in a Newborn

  • High-output congestive heart failure (due to severe arteriovenous shunting).
  • Non-communicating hydrocephalus (aqueductal compression).
  • Cranial bruit.

5Anatomical Relations of the Oculomotor Nerve (CN III)

📝 Exam Check

Identify the nerve emerging between the Posterior Cerebral Artery (PCA) and Superior Cerebellar Artery (SCA):

  1. A. Optic nerve
  2. B. Oculomotor nerve
  3. C. Trigeminal nerve
  4. D. Trochlear nerve
✔ Answer: B. Oculomotor nerve

Key Anatomical Relations on the Ventral Brainstem

  • CN III (Oculomotor): Emerges from the interpeduncular fossa of the midbrain and courses directly between the Posterior Cerebral Artery (PCA) above and the Superior Cerebellar Artery (SCA) below.

Clinical Significance

  • An aneurysm of the Posterior Communicating Artery (PCoA) or PCA/SCA compresses the outer parasympathetic fibers of CN III first
  • → pupillomotor dysfunction (blown/dilated pupil), followed by complete "down and out" ophthalmoplegia.

Other Nerves on Ventral Brainstem Inspection

  • CN II (Optic): Forms the optic chiasm anteriorly.
  • CN V (Trigeminal): The thickest cranial nerve, exiting laterally from the mid-pons.
  • CN IV (Trochlear): The only cranial nerve emerging from the dorsal aspect of the brainstem.

⚕️Acid-Base Disorders — Complete Guide

1Questions 1–3: The ROME Mnemonic & Fundamentals

Question 1 · ROME

The primary change in metabolic acidosis is:

  1. A. Increase in bicarbonate
  2. B. Decrease in pCO₂
  3. C. Decrease in bicarbonate
  4. D. Increase in pCO₂

The very first thing to learn and remember is the mnemonic ROME in acid-base disorders — it solves the majority of questions:

  • Respiratory — Opposite
  • Metabolic — Equal

Look at the changes in three parameters: pH, HCO₃⁻ (bicarbonate) and pCO₂. In compensated disorders, HCO₃⁻ and pCO₂ go in the same direction — like siblings, both rise together or both fall together.

Now correlate this with pH: in acidosis pH decreases; in alkalosis pH increases.

  • Respiratory (Opposite): in respiratory acidosis, pH decreases — so HCO₃⁻ and pCO₂ increase.
  • Metabolic (Equal): in metabolic acidosis, pH decreases — everything moves the same way, so after compensation pH ↓, HCO₃⁻ ↓, pCO₂ ↓.

How do you know what the primary change is? Respiratory literally means the problem is in the lungs (ventilation), so the pCO₂ change is primary. Metabolic means a non-lung problem (e.g., kidneys), so the HCO₃⁻ change is primary.

In this question: acidosis → pH decreases; metabolic → equal → HCO₃⁻ and pCO₂ also decrease; metabolic → the primary change is in bicarbonate.

Answer: C — Decrease in bicarbonate
Question 2 · Respiratory

Compensatory change in respiratory acidosis is:

  1. A. Increase in bicarbonate
  2. B. Decrease in pCO₂
  3. C. Decrease in bicarbonate
  4. D. Increase in pCO₂

Break it down step-by-step:

  • It is acidosis → pH decreases.
  • It is a respiratory disorder → the primary change is in pCO₂, and the compensatory change is in HCO₃⁻ (so options B and D are immediately ruled out).
  • According to ROME, respiratory is opposite. Since pH is decreasing, HCO₃⁻ (and pCO₂) will increase.
Answer: A — Increase in bicarbonate
Question 3 · Metabolic

Primary change in metabolic alkalosis is:

  1. A. Increase in bicarbonate
  2. B. Decrease in pCO₂
  3. C. Decrease in bicarbonate
  4. D. Increase in pCO₂

Break it down step-by-step:

  • It is metabolic → the primary change is in bicarbonate (options B and D, which involve pCO₂, are gone).
  • It is alkalosis → pH increases.
  • Metabolic is equal (all increase or all decrease) → bicarbonate will also increase.
Answer: A — Increase in bicarbonate

3Concept Check 2: Respiratory & Renal Compensation

In metabolic disorders you get respiratory compensation; in respiratory disorders you get metabolic (renal) compensation. Also pay attention to H⁺ vs pH: when pH decreases, H⁺ increases.

Metabolic Acidosis

  • pH ↓ (↑ H⁺), primary ↓ HCO₃⁻
  • Compensation: pCO₂ needs to decrease. How does CO₂ leave the body? By hyperventilation — this is why you see Kussmaul's breathing in Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA).

Metabolic Alkalosis

  • pH ↑ (↓ H⁺), primary ↑ HCO₃⁻
  • Compensation: pCO₂ needs to increase. How do you retain CO₂? By hypoventilation.

Respiratory Acidosis

  • pH ↓ (↑ H⁺), primary ↑ pCO₂
  • Renal compensation: there is already too much acid in the body, so the kidneys push acid out and keep alkali in → ↑ H⁺ excretion and ↑ HCO₃⁻ reabsorption.

Respiratory Alkalosis

  • pH ↑ (↓ H⁺), primary ↓ pCO₂
  • Renal compensation: the body has too much alkali, so the kidneys do the opposite → ↓ H⁺ excretion and ↓ HCO₃⁻ reabsorption.

Notice that H⁺ excretion and HCO₃⁻ reabsorption always move in the same direction!

4Questions 4–6: Causes, Anion Gap & Compensation

Question 4 · Causes

Which of the following is a cause of metabolic alkalosis?

  1. A. Diarrhea
  2. B. Chronic renal failure
  3. C. Ethylene glycol ingestion
  4. D. Treatment with acetazolamide
  5. E. Hyperaldosteronism
  6. F. Salicylate poisoning

Mnemonic: Aldosterone causes Alkalosis! And whenever there is alkalosis, remember it is "al-K-loss" → potassium (K⁺) generally decreases, so alkalosis is associated with hypokalemia.

Why the other options are incorrect — they cause acidosis:

  • (A) Diarrhea vs. vomiting: in vomiting, stomach acid goes out → alkali remains → alkalosis. In diarrhea, intestinal bicarbonate (alkali) goes out → acidosis. Mnemonic: Diarrhea → D causes aciDosis. Diarrhea causes Normal Anion Gap Metabolic Acidosis (NAGMA).
  • High-yield pearl — GI vs. renal NAGMA: causes of NAGMA are essentially "diarrhea" — either GI diarrhea or renal diarrhea (Renal Tubular Acidosis / RTA). Differentiate by checking the urinary anion gap (UAG). Urine and renal go together → UAG is positive in RTA and negative in GI diarrhea.
  • (B) Chronic renal failure: H⁺ cannot go out → acidosis.
  • (C) Ethylene glycol ingestion: exam clues are a history of antifreeze ingestion and oxaluria (oxalic acid in urine) → acidosis.
  • (D) Acetazolamide: causes acidosis. Brand name Diamox (Di = 2) → RTA Type 2.
  • (F) Salicylate poisoning: salicylate is salicylic acid → metabolic acidosis. It simultaneously stimulates the respiratory center (hyperventilation, CO₂ washout) → respiratory alkalosis as well.
Answer: E — Hyperaldosteronism
Question 5 · Anion Gap

Choose the incorrect statement about anion gap:

  1. A. In lactic acidosis anion gap is increased
  2. B. Anion gap is decreased in hypercalcemia
  3. C. Anion gap is decreased in lithium toxicity
  4. D. Anion gap is decreased in ketoacidosis

Exam strategy: when you are 100% sure one statement is clearly wrong, don't waste time overthinking the others. Everyone knows DKA is a High Anion Gap Metabolic Acidosis (HAGMA) — so saying the anion gap is decreased in ketoacidosis is definitely incorrect.

Revise the concept behind the anion gap (AG):

  • What is it? The gap due to unmeasured anions (like organic acids and phosphates).
  • Formula: AG = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻) (sometimes K⁺ is added to Na⁺).
  • Normal anion gap: 8–12 mEq/L.
  • Increased in (A) & (D): lactic acid and ketoacids are organic acids (unmeasured anions) → they increase the anion gap.
  • Decreased in (B) & (C): calcium (Ca²⁺ in hypercalcemia) and lithium (Li⁺ in lithium toxicity) are unmeasured cations. When unmeasured cations increase, the anion gap decreases.
Answer: D — Anion gap is decreased in ketoacidosis
Question 6 · Compensated Alkalosis

Which laboratory results indicate compensated metabolic alkalosis?

  1. A. Low pCO₂, normal bicarbonate, high pH
  2. B. Low pCO₂, low bicarbonate, low pH
  3. C. High pCO₂, normal bicarbonate, low pH
  4. D. High pCO₂, high bicarbonate, high pH

Use ROME: metabolic means equal (everything moves in the same direction). Since it is alkalosis, pH is high — so both bicarbonate and pCO₂ must also be high.

Answer: D — High pCO₂, high bicarbonate, high pH

  2. B. Metabolic alkalosis
  3. C. Respiratory acidosis
  4. D. Respiratory alkalosis

"Breathing rapidly" is the major clue — it is a breathing/ventilation issue, so it must be respiratory (rule out the metabolic options). Rapid breathing = hyperventilation → CO₂ is washed out (CO₂ ↓). Since respiratory is opposite (ROME), pH ↑ → respiratory alkalosis.

Question 10 · Diarrhea

A 45-year-old woman develops severe diarrhea while on vacation. pH 7.25, pCO₂ 24 mmHg, [HCO₃⁻] 10 mEq/L. Venous blood shows decreased K⁺ and a normal anion gap. What is the diagnosis?

  1. A. Metabolic acidosis
  2. B. Metabolic alkalosis
  3. C. Respiratory acidosis
  4. D. Respiratory alkalosis
  5. E. Normal acid-base status

Always check the values rather than guessing from the history alone:

  • pH 7.25 (↓)
  • pCO₂ 24 (↓ from 40)
  • HCO₃⁻ 10 (↓ from 24)

All values move in the same direction (all decreased) → metabolic acidosis — and as expected with diarrhea, it has a normal anion gap (NAGMA).

Question 11 · Coma

  1. A. Metabolic acidosis
  2. B. Metabolic alkalosis
  3. C. Respiratory acidosis
  4. D. Respiratory alkalosis

Work through it:

  • First check pH: 7.1 (↓) → acidosis (alkalosis options are out).
  • Next, check the other values: pCO₂ is 16 (↓) and HCO₃⁻ is 5 (↓).
  • Everything is decreased (equal direction) → metabolic acidosis.
Answer: A — Metabolic acidosis
Question 12 · GI Aspiration

A man undergoing surgery needed aspiration of the upper GI tract. After surgery: pH 7.55, pCO₂ 52 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ 40 mmol/L. What is the underlying disorder?

  1. A. Metabolic acidosis
  2. B. Metabolic alkalosis
  3. C. Respiratory acidosis
  4. D. Respiratory alkalosis

Work through it:

  • pH 7.55 (↑) → alkalosis (acidosis options are out).
  • pCO₂ 52 (↑ from 40), HCO₃⁻ 40 (↑ from 24).
  • All values are increased (same direction) → metabolic alkalosis — also matches loss of gastric acid.
Answer: B — Metabolic alkalosis
Question 13 · Ionized Calcium

A patient has pH 7.52, pCO₂ 20 mmHg and [HCO₃⁻] 16 mEq/L. Which statement about this patient is most likely correct?

  1. A. He is hypoventilating
  2. B. He has decreased ionized [Ca²⁺] in blood
  3. C. He has almost complete respiratory compensation
  4. D. He has an acid-base disorder caused by overproduction of fixed acid
  5. E. Appropriate renal compensation would cause arterial [HCO₃⁻] to increase

First, diagnose the disorder:

  • pH 7.52 (↑) → alkalosis.
  • pCO₂ 20 (↓), HCO₃⁻ 16 (↓).
  • Direction is opposite to pH → respiratory alkalosis.

Now evaluate each option:

  • (A) Hypoventilating: in hypoventilation CO₂ increases. Here pCO₂ is 20 (decreased), meaning he is hyperventilating. Incorrect.
  • (B) Decreased ionized [Ca²⁺]: this is the key concept — in alkalosis, H⁺ ions are busy compensating and are unavailable to bind negatively charged albumin. Albumin then binds free calcium instead, so free (ionized) calcium decreases. This is why hyperventilating patients can present with tetany. Correct.
  • (C) Almost complete respiratory compensation: this is already a respiratory disorder, so compensation is renal, not respiratory. Incorrect.
  • (D) Overproduction of fixed acid: that would cause acidosis, not alkalosis. Incorrect.
  • (E) Renal compensation would increase [HCO₃⁻]: bicarbonate is an alkali, and the patient already has alkalosis — we do not want more alkali. Renal compensation decreases HCO₃⁻ reabsorption. Incorrect.
Answer: B — Decreased ionized [Ca²⁺] in blood
Question 14 · Renal Failure

  1. A. Metabolic acidosis
  2. B. Metabolic alkalosis
  3. C. Respiratory acidosis

Question 17 · Metabolic Alkalosis Causes

Causes of metabolic alkalosis include all the following EXCEPT:

  1. A. Mineralocorticoid deficiency
  2. B. Hypokalemia
  3. C. Thiazide diuretic therapy
  4. D. Recurrent vomiting

Evaluate each option:

  • (A) Mineralocorticoid deficiency: aldosterone is decreased. Aldosterone causes alkalosis, so decreased aldosterone causes acidosis — this is the exception.
  • (B) Hypokalemia: alkalosis is "al-K-loss" (↓ K⁺).
  • (C) Thiazide diuretic therapy: causes hypokalemia → alkalosis.
  • (D) Recurrent vomiting: stomach acid goes out → alkalosis.

Which of the following conditions will NOT cause respiratory alkalosis?

  1. A. Fever
  2. B. Anxiety
  3. C. Laryngeal obstruction
  4. D. Salicylate toxicity

Evaluate each option:

  • (C) Laryngeal obstruction: the airway is blocked, so CO₂ cannot get out (↑ CO₂). This causes ↓ pH → respiratory acidosis — it will not cause alkalosis.
  • (A) Fever & (B) Anxiety: both cause tachypnea/hyperventilation → CO₂ washout → respiratory alkalosis.
  • (D) Salicylate toxicity: causes metabolic acidosis (salicylic acid) plus respiratory alkalosis (it stimulates the respiratory center).

Question 19 · Alkalosis Facts

All are true about metabolic alkalosis EXCEPT:

  1. A. Associated with hyperkalemia
  2. B. Associated with decreased ionic calcium concentration
  3. C. Can be caused by primary hyperaldosteronism
  4. D. Can be caused by a renin-secreting tumor

Evaluate each option:

  • (A) Associated with hyperkalemia: false! Remember "al-K-loss" → metabolic alkalosis is associated with hypokalemia.
  • (B) Decreased ionic calcium: true — in alkalosis, Ca²⁺ decreases → tetany.
  • (C) Primary hyperaldosteronism: true — aldosterone → alkalosis.
  • (D) Renin-secreting tumor: true — ↑ renin activates RAAS → ↑ aldosterone → alkalosis. Any cause of increased aldosterone causes alkalosis.
Answer: A — Associated with hyperkalemia
Question 20 · Renal Acid Handling

All are true for renal handling of acids in metabolic acidosis EXCEPT:

  1. A. Hydrogen ion secretion is increased
  2. B. Bicarbonate reabsorption is decreased
  3. C. Urinary acidity is increased
  4. D. Urinary ammonia is increased

Think logically: in metabolic acidosis, blood acids are already increased, so the kidneys push acid into the urine and keep alkali in the blood.

  • (C) Urinary acidity is increased: true — acid goes into the urine.
  • (A) Hydrogen ion secretion is increased: true — H⁺ is acid, secreted into urine.
  • (B) Bicarbonate reabsorption is decreased: notice that (A) and (B) are contradictory, because H⁺ secretion and HCO₃⁻ reabsorption go in the same direction. HCO₃⁻ is an alkali needed to neutralize the excess acid in blood, so bicarbonate reabsorption must increase, not decrease. This is the false statement.
  • (D) Urinary ammonia is increased: true — H⁺ secreted into urine combines with ammonia (NH₃) to form ammonium (NH₄⁺), so it is not reabsorbed.

Bonus exam pearl: urine phosphate also increases in metabolic acidosis, because phosphate acts as a buffer for the excreted H⁺ in urine.

Answer: B — Bicarbonate reabsorption is decreased
Question 21 · Mixed Disorder

A patient with CKD presents with vomiting and diarrhea. Blood gas: pH 7.4, HCO₃⁻ 23 mEq/L, Na⁺ 145 mEq/L, Cl⁻ 100 mEq/L. What is the probable diagnosis?

  1. A. No acid-base abnormality
  2. B. Respiratory acidosis
  3. C. Metabolic alkalosis
  4. D. HAGMA with metabolic alkalosis

Do not get trapped when an exam question gives a normal pH of 7.4! Notice that along with pH 7.4, the examiner specifically gave Na⁺, Cl⁻ and HCO₃⁻ values — because they want you to calculate the anion gap:

AG = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻) = 145 − (100 + 23) = 22
  • Normal anion gap is 8–12, so an AG of 22 is increased.
  • This indicates a High Anion Gap Metabolic Acidosis (HAGMA) from CKD, but the pH is normalized to 7.4 because there is a mixed acid-base disorder — specifically metabolic alkalosis (from vomiting) balancing it out. These mixed-disorder questions used to be frequently asked in AIIMS exams.
Answer: D — HAGMA with metabolic alkalosis

7Concept Check 3: Predicted Compensation & Winter's Formula

Let's look at the exact numbers for compensation. Remember:

  • Metabolic disorders have no acute/chronic division (respiratory compensation happens early).
  • Respiratory disorders are divided into acute and chronic.

1. Per 1-Unit vs. Per 10-Unit Change

Memorise this sequence of numbers for every 10-unit change: "13, 6, 1, 4, 2, 4" (13 and its rough half 6, then 1–4, 2–4).

2. Winter's Formula (Metabolic Acidosis)

Another way to calculate predicted PaCO₂ in metabolic acidosis is Winter's formula:

PaCO₂ = (1.5 × HCO₃⁻) + 8 ± 2

Practice Calculation

Suppose a patient has metabolic acidosis and their HCO₃⁻ drops to 14 mEq/L. What should their pCO₂ be for full compensation?

  • Method 1 (13-rule): normal HCO₃⁻ is 24, so it decreased by 10 (24 − 14 = 10). In metabolic acidosis, for every 10 decrease in HCO₃⁻, pCO₂ decreases by 13. Normal pCO₂ is 40 → 40 − 13 = 27 mmHg.
  • Method 2 (Winter's formula): (1.5 × 14) + 8 ± 2 = 21 + 8 ± 2 = 29 ± 2 mmHg (27–31 mmHg).

1GIT — Upper GIT & Pediatric Obstructions

1. Achalasia Cardia

  • Barium swallow shows a dilated proximal esophagus with smooth distal tapering at the gastroesophageal junction — the classic "Bird beak sign".
  • Why does this happen? The lower esophageal sphincter (LES) remains contracted and fails to relax.
  • Clinical pearl: dysphagia is characteristically more to liquids than solids, because heavy solids force open the contracted LES while liquids cannot.
  • Investigations: gold standard = manometry. (Trap: 24-hour pH monitoring is for acid reflux/GERD, not achalasia.)
  • Exam mnemonic (aCHALasia): CH → Chicago classification & Chagas disease (Trypanosoma cruzi); HAL → Heller’s myotomy (also POEM — Peroral Endoscopic Myotomy).
  • Image interpretation: fluoroscopic single-contrast barium swallow of the distal esophagus and esophagogastric junction (AP/oblique). Smooth, concentric, symmetric tapering ("bird beak") without mucosal irregularity or shouldering — this distinguishes it from malignant pseudoachalasia (carcinoma esophagus).

2. Diffuse Esophageal Spasm (DES)

  • Barium swallow shows multiple uncoordinated contractions creating a "Corkscrew esophagus" appearance.
  • Clinical trigger: chest pain triggered after eating hot or spicy food.
  • Image interpretation: segmented, non-peristaltic tertiary contractions along the mid-to-distal esophageal body — also called the rosary-bead appearance.

3. Congenital Hypertrophic Pyloric Stenosis (CHPS / IHPS)

  • Barium meal (remember: meal = stomach) shows a narrowed, elongated pyloric canal → "String sign" of the pylorus.
  • Ultrasound (transverse view of pylorus) shows an echogenic central mucosa surrounded by a hypoechoic thickened muscle ring → "Target sign" or "Doughnut sign".
  • Presentation: around 2–4 weeks of age (not immediately at birth) in a first-born male child with non-bilious projectile vomiting (obstruction is proximal to the ampulla of Vater).
  • Pathology: hypertrophy of the circular muscle layer (not longitudinal).
  • Drug association: erythromycin exposure in neonates.
  • IOC: ultrasound (avoids radiation in infants). Cutoffs: muscle thickness > 4 mm and channel length > 16 mm (easy rule: 4 and 4²). If thickness ≤ 4 mm with similar symptoms → pylorospasm.
  • Management: it is a medical emergency, NOT a surgical emergency. First step = correct fluid and electrolyte imbalance; definitive surgery = Ramstedt’s pyloromyotomy (mnemonic: male child "Ram").
  • Image interpretation: on barium meal, contrast fills a distended stomach with an abrupt cutoff and a thin streak through the hypertrophied pylorus. On transverse grey-scale USG, a thick hypoechoic muscularis (> 4 mm) encircles an echogenic mucosal core.

4. Duodenal Atresia & Jejunal Atresia

  • Erect abdominal/chest X-ray in a neonate shows two large black air bubbles (distended stomach + proximal duodenum) with absent distal bowel gas → "Double bubble sign".
  • Mnemonic (D-D-D): Double bubble → Duodenal atresia → Down syndrome. Also seen in annular pancreas.
  • Exam trap (FMGE July ’24): three air bubbles (stomach + duodenum + jejunum) → "Triple bubble sign" = jejunal atresia.
  • Image interpretation: two distinct gas-filled lucencies in the upper abdomen (larger left-sided stomach bubble, smaller right-sided duodenal bulb) with a completely gasless lower abdomen → complete high GI obstruction.

2GIT — Lower GIT & Intussusception

1. Carcinoma Colon

  • On barium enema, the colon is identified by haustrations. The transverse colon shows a short, irregular stricture with overhanging edges (shouldering) → "Apple core sign" (Core = Carcinoma colon).

2. Sigmoid Volvulus

  • Plain abdominal X-ray shows a massively dilated loop rising from the pelvis with a central cleft → "Coffee bean sign".
  • Causes Large Bowel Obstruction (LBO).
  • Barium enema shows a tapered twist → "Bird beak sign" or "Bird of prey sign".

3. Colonic Diverticulosis vs. Diverticulitis

  • Barium enema shows multiple barium-filled outpouchings along the sigmoid/descending colon → "Saw-tooth appearance".
  • Diverticulosis (asymptomatic outpouchings): IOC = barium enema.
  • Diverticulitis (acute inflammation, pain in LIF since sigmoid is the MC site): barium is contraindicated → IOC = CECT; staged by Hinchey’s classification.

4. Ulcerative Colitis (UC)

  • Barium enema shows a rigid, narrowed, featureless descending colon with complete loss of haustrations → "Lead pipe colon".
  • Toxic megacolon is also a classic complication of UC.

5. Intussusception

  • Clinical correlation: weaning-age child (viral-induced lymphoid/Peyer’s patch hyperplasia) presenting with red currant jelly stools (blood + mucus).
  • IOC: ultrasound in a child (avoids radiation); CECT in an adult (to look for a pathological lead point).
  • Supine X-ray: ill-defined sausage-shaped soft-tissue mass on the right side.
  • Transverse USG: bowel-within-bowel concentric rings → "Target sign" / "Doughnut sign".
  • Barium enema: meniscus-shaped filling defect → "Claw sign"; when contrast outlines mucosal folds between the intussusceptum and intussuscipiens → "Coiled spring sign".
  • Management: pneumatic reduction (air enema) or hydrostatic reduction (saline enema — not barium).

3Hepatobiliary-Pancreatic (HBP) & Renal Calculi

1. Hydatid Cyst of Liver

  • Radioanatomy orientation (CECT abdomen): spine/vertebra is white; anteriorly is the aorta; immediately to the right of the aorta is the IVC (NOT the gallbladder, which is superficial/anterior and grey); on the left is the stomach showing jet-black air above and grey fluid below with gastric rugae.
  • Pathology: large cystic (grey) lesion in the right lobe of the liver containing undulating, detached floating membranes → "Water lily sign" (pathognomonic of partially ruptured hydatid cyst).
  • Trap: amebic liver abscess appears uniform grey without floating membranes. HIDA scan is for biliary tract patency/atresia/bile leak.

2. Cholelithiasis & Choledocholithiasis

  • Ultrasound gallbladder: fluid (bile) appears black on USG. Echogenic calculi cast dark shadows behind them → Posterior Acoustic Shadowing (PAS).
  • MRCP: bright white gallbladder and CBD (no white bone, no endoscope) with a dark rounded filling defect at the distal CBD → CBD stone.
  • Gallstones: IOC = ultrasound.
  • CBD stone: IOC (diagnostic) = MRCP; therapeutic + gold standard = ERCP.

3. Renal Stone

  • Ultrasound kidney: echogenic calculus inside the renal pelvis casting Posterior Acoustic Shadowing (PAS).
  • Physics rule: bad conductors of sound (stones, bone, air) block ultrasound waves → posterior shadowing. Good conductors (fluid) → posterior acoustic enhancement.
  • IOC: NCCT KUB (renal stones are calcified; gallstones are radiolucent/best on USG).

4. Acute Pancreatitis & Pancreatic Pseudocyst

  • CECT abdomen: large, well-defined, walled-off grey fluid collection anterior to the pancreas → pancreatic pseudocyst.
  • Definition: occurs in non-necrotizing (interstitial edematous) pancreatitis > 4 weeks after onset.
  • IOC for acute pancreatitis: CECT (bulky pancreas; chronic pancreatitis shows an atrophic pancreas).

4Emergency Radiology

1. Pulmonary Embolism (PE)

  • Anatomy review (CTPA / CECT chest): bone posterior to the ribcage = scapula. Great vessels from right to left (SAAP): SVC → Ascending Aorta → Pulmonary Artery. Posteriorly next to the vertebra = descending aorta. Jet-black air-filled bifurcated tubes = right and left main bronchi (the esophagus is collapsed and not air-distended).
  • Finding: large dark filling defect (non-opacified thrombus) straddling the pulmonary artery bifurcation.
  • Clinical correlation: immobilization, long travel, recent surgery, calf pain/edema (DVT).
  • Workup: screening = D-dimer; IOC = CTPA (or CECT if CTPA is absent from options); clinical score = Wells criteria.

2. Pneumoperitoneum

  • Erect chest X-ray: free black crescentic air under the right hemidiaphragm → hollow viscus/bowel perforation.
  • Supine abdominal X-ray (neonate): giant central oval lucency outlining the falciform ligament → "Football sign" (massive pneumoperitoneum).
  • Best X-ray: CXR erect. If the patient cannot stand → left lateral decubitus with a horizontal beam.
  • Contrast rule: barium is contraindicated in suspected perforation → use water-soluble iodinated contrast (Iohexol or Gastrografin).

3. Pneumothorax

  • Chest X-ray PA: jet-black pleural space devoid of peripheral lung vascular markings, demarcated by a sharp visceral pleural line around the collapsed lung.
  • Clinical findings: hyper-resonant percussion note + decreased breath sounds.
  • Management: not all pneumothoraces need needle decompression — perform needle puncture followed by ICD only in tension pneumothorax (hemodynamically unstable).
  • M-mode USG signs: Barcode sign / Stratosphere sign, Lung point sign.

4. Diffuse Axonal Injury (DAI)

  • MRI brain — SWI (Susceptibility Weighted Imaging): bone cortex is black → MRI. Tiny black petechial hemorrhages at the grey-white matter junction.
  • SWI is the most sensitive MRI sequence for hemorrhage and calcification.
  • Clinical trap: trauma patient with poor GCS and a normal CT scan → suspect DAI → IOC = MRI (SWI).

8Modality Identification & MRI Sequences

  • CT knee: look at the bone cortex (bright white → CT), NOT the marrow. Why is the marrow dark here? Because it is fatty marrow, and fat has a negative HU value so it appears dark on CT.
  • MRI brain sequences (bone is black):
  • T2 (WW2): Water White → CSF in ventricles and sulci is bright white.
  • T1 (1st year = Anatomy): anatomy is followed → white matter is white (corpus callosum, internal capsule), grey matter is grey, CSF is black.
  • FLAIR: all black (CSF is suppressed/black, white matter is darker than grey).
  • BMFT (Barium Meal Follow-Through): visualizes stomach + small bowel loops. Feathery mucosal appearance in the left upper quadrant = jejunum (valvulae conniventes).
  • PET-CT: orange-colored hybrid/fusion imaging. PET is a nuclear medicine functional imaging modality.
  • Tracer: 18F-FDG (positron emitter; t½ ≈ 110 min or ~2 hours), based on the Warburg effect (increased glucose uptake by tumor cells). Has radiation exposure.

14Lobar Pathologies, Pericarditis & CHD

  • Right middle lobe (RML) consolidation/collapse: lateral CXR shows wedge opacity bounded by horizontal and oblique fissures; PA CXR shows silhouette sign obliterating the right heart border (RHB).
  • Lingular pathology: silhouette sign obliterating the left heart border (LHB) (there is no left middle lobe).
  • Calcific constrictive pericarditis: curvilinear white calcification encasing the heart on CXR.
  • JVP: M-pattern (X and Y both present, prominent Y descent).
  • Signs (KK): Kussmaul sign, pericardial knock.
  • Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF): boot-shaped heart on CXR (due to RVH and pulmonary stenosis; oligemic lungs).

15Genitourinary (GU) Radiology

  • Polycystic kidney disease (PCKD): bilateral enlarged kidneys replaced by cysts — grey on CT, bright white on T2 MRI.
  • Horseshoe kidney: kidneys fused across the midline anterior to the aorta → associated with Turner syndrome.
  • Putty kidney: dense white lobular renal calcification on CT and plain X-ray → renal TB (classic history: sterile pyuria).
  • PUJ obstruction: IVP (20-min film) showing left hydronephrosis with non-visualized left ureter (no hydroureter).
  • Vesicoureteric reflux (VUR): urethra is seen + bladder is completely distended, and contrast ascends into ureter/kidney during voiding → MCU showing VUR.
  • IOC for VUR & PUV: MCU. History: child with recurrent UTI.
  • IOC for renal scarring: DMSA scan (S = Scar).

16Obstetrics & Gynecology Imaging

  • NB–NT scan: performed at 11–13 weeks (1st-trimester Down syndrome screening). NT > 3 mm is abnormal → confirm by karyotyping.
  • Complete molar pregnancy: USG showing multiple cystic spaces without fetal parts → "Snowstorm appearance".
  • PCOS: peripherally arranged follicles (2–9 mm, no dominant follicle) around thickened androgen-dependent central stroma → "String of pearls / Necklace sign".
  • HSG — unicornuate uterus: X-ray contrast study (has radiation, done around Day 9). Contrast fills a single "Banana-shaped uterus".
  • Uterine anomalies workup: 3D USG → MRI → hysteroscopy + laparoscopy (gold standard).
  • Bicornuate vs. septate uterus: cannot be reliably separated by HSG alone (requires MRI). Bicornuate (bi-complete / bi-big): outer fundal cleft, big angle; septate (s-smooth / s-small): smooth outer contour, small angle.

17Thyroid Scan, Proton Bragg Peak & Bone Dysplasias

  • Thyroid scintigraphy: black = hot. Diffuse increased uptake = Graves disease; diffuse decreased uptake = thyroiditis.
  • Bragg peak: characteristic of proton beam therapy (BR–PR), depositing maximum energy precisely at the tumor depth while sparing surrounding tissues.
  • Hemolytic anemia skull X-ray: vertical diploic trabeculations → "Hair-on-end" / crew-cut appearance (sickle cell anemia, thalassemia, G6PD deficiency).
  • Fibrous dysplasia: fib = femur bent (Shepherd’s crook deformity) with ground-glass matrix.
  • McCune–Albright syndrome: 3 Ps — precocious puberty, polyostotic fibrous dysplasia, pigmentation (café-au-lait spots).
  • Mazabraud syndrome: fibrous dysplasia + myxoma.

🖐️Concepts & Ulnar Nerve Tests

1Foundational Motor Innervation

Before memorising tests, build the anatomical foundation — every bedside test in this topic is simply a way of isolating one of three nerve territories.

1. The Rule of Hand Muscle Innervation

  • Ulnar nerve (“musician’s nerve”): supplies ALL intrinsic muscles of the hand EXCEPT the thenar muscles and the lateral two lumbricals.
  • Median nerve takes: thenar muscles — abductor pollicis brevis, superficial head of flexor pollicis brevis, opponens pollicis — plus the 1st and 2nd (lateral two) lumbricals.
  • Flexor compartment of forearm & fingers: lateral three digits (thumb, index, middle) flexors → median nerve; medial two digits (ring, little) flexors → ulnar nerve.
  • Extensor compartment: all extensors of the wrist, thumb and fingers → radial nerve and its deep branch, the posterior interosseous nerve (PIN).

2. Two Crucial Thumb Muscles Supplied by the Ulnar Nerve

  • Although the thumb is mostly median-nerve territory, two key thumb muscles belong to the ulnar nerve. Think of the ulnar nerve pulling the thumb medially toward the ring and little fingers:
  • Adductor pollicis
  • Deep head of flexor pollicis brevis (FPB)
  • Why it matters: losing these two — not thenar wasting — is what produces a positive Froment’s sign.

3. Interossei & Lumbricals: Actions & Innervation

  • Interossei (Unterossei = Ulnar nerve): ALL interossei, palmar and dorsal, are supplied by the ulnar nerve.
  • Palmar interossei: 3 muscles, unipennate → PAD = Palmar ADduction of the fingers.
  • Dorsal interossei: 4 muscles, bipennate → DAB = Dorsal ABduction of the fingers.
  • Lumbricals (LUMbricals = Ulnar + Median): contain both U and M → medial two supplied by the ulnar nerve, lateral two by the median nerve.
  • Action (think of the letter L): lumbricals place the hand in an inverted-L position — flexion at the MCP joint + extension at the IP joints.
  • Why this matters clinically: when lumbricals are paralysed, unopposed action causes hyperextension at the MCP joint + flexion at the IP joints → claw hand.

3Ulnar Nerve Integrity Tests

1. Book Test & Froment’s Sign

  • How it is performed: ask the patient to hold a book or a piece of paper flat between the thumb and the index finger — this tests adduction of the thumb.
  • Normal (negative) response: the adductor pollicis (ulnar nerve) keeps the thumb straight/extended and firmly adducted against the book.
  • Abnormal (positive) response: when the ulnar nerve is paralysed, adductor pollicis fails. To stop the book slipping, the patient’s flexor pollicis longus (FPL) — supplied by the median nerve / anterior interosseous nerve — substitutes by flexing the IP joint of the thumb. This is Froment’s sign.
  • Visual clue: in addition to thumb IP flexion, look for wasting/hollowing of the 1st dorsal interosseous in the first web space — a hallmark confirming ulnar nerve involvement.
  • Image interpretation: the normal hand grips the book/card with a flat, extended thumb IP joint. In the positive test the distal phalanx of the thumb bends sharply, due to compensatory FPL recruitment, with marked 1st dorsal interosseous wasting and compensatory hyperextension at the thumb MCP joint (Jeanne’s sign).

2. Card Test (Card – PAD)

  • How it is performed: a flat card is placed between two adjacent extended fingers and the examiner tries to pull it out while the patient holds it tight.
  • What it tests: holding the card requires pressing the fingers together (ADduction), so it tests the palmar interossei — PAD, ulnar nerve.
  • Memory link: Card and PAD rhyme.
  • Image interpretation: the examiner slides a thin card between the patient’s extended digits (e.g., ring and little fingers) with the palm flat, isolating palmar interossei adduction strength without allowing finger flexion to compensate.

3. Egawa Test (DE: Dorsal Interossei – Egawa)

  • How it is performed: the patient places the palm flat on a table and attempts to abduct the fingers (spread them apart against resistance, or move the middle finger side to side).
  • What it tests: spreading the fingers is ABduction, so it tests the dorsal interossei — DAB, ulnar nerve.
  • Memory link: Dorsal interossei – Egawa (D and E in ABCDEF).
  • Image interpretation: the examiner’s index finger applies lateral resistance against the radial/ulnar border of the patient’s abducted finger while the hand rests flat, isolating dorsal interosseous muscle power.

💪Median, Radial & Claw Hand

4Median Nerve Integrity Tests

1. Pointing Index / Ochsner’s Clasping Test

  • How it is performed: ask the patient to clasp both hands tightly together (Ochsner’s clasping test).
  • Finding: the index finger fails to flex and remains straight/extended, pointing outward → pointing index (P = median nerve).
  • Why does this happen? The flexors of the index finger — flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS) plus the lateral half of flexor digitorum profundus (FDP) — are supplied by the median nerve. Even if the middle finger can partly flex through deep ulnar slips or remains weak, the classic pointing digit is the index finger.
  • Image interpretation: interlocked fingers where the ulnar digits (middle, ring, little) curl around the opposite hand, while the affected index finger stands fully extended across both MCP and IP joints.

2. Pincer Grasp / Kiloh-Nevin Sign

  • How it is performed: ask the patient to touch the tip of the thumb to the tip of the index finger to form a round “O” (pincer grasp).
  • Normal: flexion at both the thumb IP joint (FPL) and index DIP joint (lateral half of FDP) produces a clean tip-to-tip circle.
  • Abnormal (Kiloh-Nevin sign positive): because FPL and the lateral half of FDP are supplied by the anterior interosseous nerve (AIN) — a pure motor branch of the median nerve — the patient cannot flex the terminal phalanges. Instead of a round “O”, the pads of the extended thumb and index finger collapse flat against each other.
  • Mnemonic: “Ye Lo” → Kiloh-Nevin sign for pincer grasp.
  • Image interpretation: side-by-side photographs — the normal round “OK” sign shows clear flexion at the index DIP and thumb IP joints, while the affected hand shows hyperextension/flattening of both joints giving a flat pulp-to-pulp pinch.

3. Ape Hand / Ape Thumb Deformity

  • Marked wasting and flattening of the thenar eminence (DR. CUMA: Median → Ape).
  • The thumb falls backward into the same plane as the other fingers because the thenar muscles (median nerve) are atrophied.
  • Image interpretation: palmar view shows severe concavity and wrinkling over the lateral palmar aspect, with loss of the normal convex bulk of the thenar eminence.

4. Pope’s Benediction Sign (Benediction Test)

  • Crucial exam distinction: unlike claw hand (visible at rest), Pope’s benediction sign is elicited ONLY when you ask the patient to make a fist.
  • Mechanism: on attempted fist closure the patient can flex the medial two fingers (ring and little — ulnar nerve intact), but the thumb, index and middle fingers fail to flex and stay extended, resembling a priest/Pope giving a blessing.
  • Nerve: median nerve (P for Pope’s = median).
  • Image interpretation: dual panels — a normal tightly clenched fist versus attempted fist closure in which digits 4 and 5 flex into the palm while digits 1, 2 and 3 remain extended.

5. Pen Test

  • How it is performed: the patient lays the dorsum of the hand flat on a table (palm facing upward). A pen is held horizontally slightly above the palm and the patient is asked to lift the thumb vertically upward to touch the pen.
  • Muscle tested: abductor pollicis brevis (APB) — palmar abduction of the thumb.
  • Nerve: median nerve (P for Pen = median).
  • Image interpretation: supine hand with a pen held vertically above the thumb; upward palmar abduction of the thumb directly isolates APB without extensor pollicis interference.

5Radial Nerve & PIN Injury (Drop)

  • Main radial nerve injury: loss of wrist and finger extensors → wrist drop (the wrist hangs flexed; the patient cannot extend the wrist).
  • Posterior interosseous nerve (PIN) injury: the radial nerve travels posteriorly and its deep branch is the PIN. Injury to the PIN alone causes thumb drop and finger drop (distal extensor loss) with NO wrist drop, because extensor carpi radialis longus (ECRL) is spared.
  • Exam filter: “can still actively dorsiflex the wrist” = PIN, not a radial nerve lesion in the spiral groove.
  • Image interpretation: forearms held outstretched — the normal limb keeps wrist and fingers extended horizontally, while the affected limb shows marked palmar flexion at the wrist (wrist drop) with limp, flexed fingers.

6Claw Hand: Complete vs Partial (At Rest)

  • Complete claw hand: all fingers show MCP hyperextension and IP flexion at rest.
  • Nerves injured: ulnar + median (all four lumbricals lost).
  • Classic cause: Klumpke’s palsy — lower brachial plexus injury, C8–T1 (e.g., difficult breech delivery).
  • Partial ulnar claw hand: only the ring and little fingers are clawed at rest → ulnar nerve palsy (medial two lumbricals lost).
  • Partial median claw hand: only the index and middle fingers are clawed at rest → median nerve palsy (lateral two lumbricals lost).
  • Image interpretation: three distinct resting postures — (1) hyperextension at MCP and flexion at IP of all digits (complete claw); (2) clawing strictly of digits 4 and 5 (ulnar claw); (3) clawing strictly of digits 2 and 3 (median claw).

7Orthotic Splints & Froment’s vs Kiloh-Nevin Trap

1. Orthotic Splints (High-Yield Orthopedic Integration)

  • Radial nerve palsy (wrist drop): you want to pull the dropped wrist up into extension → cock-up splint (dynamic cock-up splint).
  • Claw hand (ulnar / median lumbrical palsy): because the lumbricals cannot flex the MCP joints (“knuckles”), you want to bend the knuckles into flexion → knuckle-bender splint.
  • Trap: wrist drop never gets a knuckle-bender; claw hand never gets a cock-up splint.

2. Resolving the Classic Exam Trap: Froment’s vs Kiloh-Nevin

  • Why students confuse them: both involve pinching between the thumb and index finger.
  • Froment’s sign (ulnar nerve): holding a book/paper → the thumb IP flexes abnormally because FPL (median) compensates for the paralysed adductor pollicis (ulnar). Look for 1st dorsal interosseous wasting in the vignette or image.
  • Kiloh-Nevin sign (median / AIN): making a pincer “O” → the patient cannot flex the thumb IP (FPL) or index DIP (FDP), so the tips flatten rather than curving into a circle.

9Image-Based Milestone Review (High-Yield Pictures)

Every milestone target exam image question falls into one of these:

  • Cruising (walking holding sofa/furniture) → 10–11 months — just before walking with support at 12 months.
  • Mouthing (putting everything into mouth) → 5–6 months — same age as monosyllables.
  • Drinking from a cup → 12 months.
  • Scribbling on paper → 15 months — same age as jargon & tower of 2 blocks.
  • Pivoting (sitting & turning around backwards to pick up a toy) → 10–11 months.
  • Bidextrous grasp (sup grip reaching with both hands) → 4 months (palm → radial).
  • Mature pincer grasp → 12 months — progression: palmar → radial → immature pincer (9m) → mature pincer (12m).
  • Tripod sitting (propped on own hands in front) → 6 months.

11Exam-Check MCQs (Classic Patterns)

🎯 Exam Check

A child sits with support, reaches for objects with one hand, speaks monosyllables and has stranger anxiety. His approximate age is:

  1. A. 3 months
  2. B. 6 months
  3. C. 9 months
  4. D. 12 months
Answer: B — 6 months

Why: All four 6-month anchors converge on ONE answer — "SIX" — sits (tripod), unidextrous grasp, monosyllables, stranger anxiety.

🎯 Exam Check

A child of proven age is able to draw a circle, ride a tricycle, alternate feet on stairs, and has a 3-word vocabulary. He is most likely:

  1. A. 18 months
  2. B. 2 years
  3. C. 3 years
  4. D. 4 years
Answer: C

Why: "Tree = Three" — circle & tricycle & alternating feet all point to 3 years.

🎯 Exam Check

A 4-year-old boy cannot hop on one foot, but can skip, build a 6-block tower, and climb stairs using alternate feet. The expected age for skipping is:

  1. A. 4 years
  2. B. 5 years
  3. C. 6 years
  4. D. 7 years
Answer: B

Why: Letter-count trick — HOPS = 4 letters (4 y); SKIPS = 5 letters (5 y).

🎯 Exam Check

During examination of ventral suspension, a neonate shows head in line with the body plane. His approximate age is:

  1. A. 1 month
  2. B. 2 months
  3. C. 3 months
  4. D. 4 months
Answer: B

Why: Ventral suspension ladder — head below = 1 m, in line = 2 m, above = 3 m.

🎯 Exam Check

A 15-month-old child can do all of the following EXCEPT:

  1. A. Walks without support
  2. B. Tower of 2 blocks
  3. C. Scribbles
  4. D. Walks up and down stairs 2 feet per step
Answer: D

Why: Both-feet-per-step stairs = 2 years ("2 for 2"). 15 months: walks alone (15), tower 2, scribbles, jargon.
